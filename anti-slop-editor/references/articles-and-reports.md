# Articles, reports, summaries, and correspondence

## Articles and essays

Identify the reader's actual question and the piece's claim. Choose an opening
that supplies an answer, a concrete sourced tension, or an appropriate scene.
Do not invent a scene or eyewitness encounter. A narrative opening need not be
replaced by an executive summary.

Give each section a distinct contribution. Support important assertions with
examples, mechanisms, evidence, or clearly labeled reasoning. A longer piece
needs depth, not a repeated thesis under new headings. Preserve deliberate
ambiguity in literary work; resolve accidental ambiguity in explanatory prose.

Let transitions express the real relation between ideas. Put counterevidence
where it changes interpretation. Do not add a generic "challenges and future"
section unless specific challenges or plans matter. End with an earned
implication, a relevant unresolved question, or narrative closure.

## Reports and memos

Establish the reporting period, audience, question or decision, and evidence
boundary. Separate what happened, why it may have happened, and what is proposed.
Put the decision or finding early when that serves the report's readers.

Use comparisons with consistent periods, units, and populations. Distinguish
actuals, targets, forecasts, scenarios, and assumptions. Preserve unfavorable
results. Do not turn missing data into zero or an unknown outcome into success.

For recommendations, identify a proposed action, its rationale, constraints,
and owner or deadline only when known or explicitly proposed. Do not invent
accountability assignments. Show uncertainty where it changes the decision.

Use a table when readers must compare values; prose when readers need to follow
a causal argument. An executive summary may repeat a key finding for navigation;
that functional repetition is not redundant filler. Keep required section
structures even when they look conventional.

## Scientific and technical writing

Preserve method, population, sample size, outcome definition, effect direction,
uncertainty, and limitations. Keep accepted terminology and appropriate passive
voice. A non-significant result is not proof of no effect. A preliminary finding
is not a general conclusion. Never manufacture citations, novelty, consensus,
or a research contribution to strengthen a manuscript.

## Summaries

Match the requested level of compression. Preserve the source's balance and
attribution, not every sentence. State a limitation when only an excerpt or part
of a document was available. Do not fill unseen sections with plausible content.
Keep the strongest relevant caveat even when it makes the summary less tidy.

## Email, letters, and social posts

Make the purpose, necessary context, and requested action findable. Keep courtesy
that suits the relationship; do not turn every professional email into blunt
commands. Do not add a deadline, threat, apology, endorsement, or commitment that
the sender did not authorize. Use a subject line when requested or appropriate.

For social writing, keep real personality and platform constraints. Do not add
fake controversy, manufactured personal lessons, staged rhetorical questions,
or engagement bait as a default. A thread, list, or rhetorical flourish is
allowed when the content and user's voice genuinely call for it.

## Review questions

Can the intended reader explain the point? Can they distinguish evidence from
interpretation? Does each retained detail do useful work? Has any editing made
the piece less true, less balanced, or less usable? Fix the specific failure
rather than shortening everything uniformly.
